Seven ideas with a real wedge
1. EPR verification for the informal recycling economy
India's Extended Producer Responsibility rules legally force brands to prove they recovered X tonnes of plastic. The recovery actually happens through informal waste pickers, but there's no auditable trail, so the credit market runs on paperwork nobody trusts. Build a system where a picker photographs a load; Gemini vision classifies polymer type and estimates weight against a reference object, cross-checks against declared volumes, flags anomalous patterns (same pile photographed twice, impossible density), and mints a signed recovery certificate. Brands get verifiable credits, pickers get proven volume and better pricing.
Wedge: fraud detection in a real compliance market, not a feel-good tracker. Money is attached. Almost nobody builds for this stakeholder.
2. Repair copilot for the informal repair sector
Extending a device's life avoids far more emissions than recycling it. Repair shops across JAPAC have skill but no schematics. Build a tool where a technician points a camera at an open appliance or PCB; Gemini identifies components and failure signatures from burn marks and bulged capacitors, reasons over a knowledge base of common failure modes, and walks the repair in the local language. Run a distilled Gemma model on-device so it works in a shop with no connectivity, syncing to Cloud Run when online.
Wedge: hybrid Gemma-local / Gemini-cloud architecture is a strong technical merit story, and the demo is visually gripping.
3. Pre-monsoon drain audit from citizen street video
Urban flooding in Kerala, Jakarta, Manila is largely a blocked-drainage problem that cities discover only after the water rises. Someone drives a scooter with a phone mounted; the pipeline geolocates frames, and Gemini vision scores each drain inlet for blockage, encroachment, and silt level. An agent layer fuses this with elevation and rainfall forecasts to rank which 50 inlets to clear first for maximum flood-risk reduction, then generates municipal work orders with cost estimates.
Wedge: it produces a prioritised action plan, not another alert dashboard. Directly hits resilience and resource efficiency.
4. Acoustic ecosystem watch
Cheap recorders in a forest, streaming to Cloud Run. Gemini's audio understanding identifies species calls, and more importantly detects chainsaws, vehicles, and gunshots. The agentic part: when an intrusion is detected, it correlates against satellite deforestation alerts and recent permit filings, then auto-generates an evidence packet (timestamped audio, location, corroborating imagery, a written incident summary) formatted for a forest officer to act on.
Wedge: audio is a badly underexploited modality in hackathons, and "evidence packet generation" is a genuinely novel use of generative AI. Western Ghats gives you a credible local story.
5. Non-revenue water leak triage
Utilities across Asia lose 30–50% of treated water to leaks before it reaches anyone. Detection today needs expensive acoustic correlators. Field staff walk a route recording pipe audio on a phone; the model classifies leak signatures against ambient noise, and an agent builds a repair route optimised by estimated litres saved per repair-hour. Show the water and energy saved per kilometre surveyed.
Wedge: huge, boring, unglamorous problem with enormous impact numbers. Judges respect a team that picked the unsexy one.
6. Adversarial ESG claim auditor
An agent team that ingests a company's sustainability report and then tries to falsify it. One agent extracts every quantitative claim, another searches news, court records, and regulatory filings for contradictions, a third checks physical claims against satellite evidence, and a referee agent produces a claim-by-claim credibility ledger with citations and a confidence score.
Wedge: adversarial multi-agent design shows off long context and grounding. This is the idea most likely to land with the industry experts on the panel, since greenwashing risk is a live boardroom concern.
7. Regenerative practice MRV for smallholders
Smallholder farmers are locked out of carbon and biodiversity markets because verification costs more than the credits are worth. Build a phone-based MRV flow: geotagged photos of residue cover, intercropping, and bunding, plus a short voice interview in the local language, fused with Sentinel imagery to confirm the practice actually happened on that plot. Output an audit-ready verification dossier at a fraction of current cost.
Wedge: attacks the cost of verification rather than adding another advisory chatbot to agriculture.

What Haven actually is
Three modules for women in abusive relationships: a steganographic SOS (keywords → LLM expands to a full distress message → AI generates an innocent image → message hidden in pixels → posted publicly with a hashtag → authority-side cron job scrapes, decodes, severity-ranks), an avatar therapy bot, and a RAG law bot over the Indian Constitution. Plus culprit matching via vector similarity to link repeat offenders across cases. Won 1st at MongoDB's "Code for a Cause."
Why it won (this is the part you should copy)
It solved a constraint, not an information gap. Almost every social-impact hackathon project assumes the user's problem is that they don't know something, so the answer is a chatbot. Haven identified that the woman knows exactly what she needs and is physically prevented from asking. Everything else follows from that reframe.
One sentence a judge can repeat. "A hidden cry for help inside a photo of a flower." Judges deliberate after seeing 40 projects. The one they can restate to each other wins.
Two-sided, not one-sided. Victim app and responder console with triage. Most teams build only the first half, which reads as a demo. Building the second half reads as a system.
Cross-case intelligence that compounds. Culprit matching turns isolated reports into a network. That's the "gets more valuable with scale" story, and it's what convinces a judge this isn't a weekend toy.
Where Haven is weak (beat it here)
Steganography over social media is fragile in reality; platforms recompress images and destroy LSB payloads. A public hashtag known to authorities is also known to abusers. Two of the three modules are commodity RAG chatbots. And there's no escalation path past a MongoDB row, no consent model, no false-positive handling. Also, Render and Vercel wouldn't qualify for this cup at all.
The reusable formula: a population blocked from acting by a constraint (surveillance, illiteracy, retaliation, distance, no data) + a channel that fits their actual physical reality + a responder console that triages and links cases + Gen AI doing the translation between the two.
Five ideas on that skeleton
1. The environmental whistleblower who can't be traced
Constraint: The person who knows a factory releases effluent at 2am, or that sand mining is happening in a protected riverbed, is a worker or villager. Reporting means retaliation. Anonymity forms don't help, because in a village of 300 people, how you write and what you know identifies you instantly.
The mechanic: Gemini rewrites the report to destroy identity signal. It strips dialect, idiolect, and spelling patterns, generalises timestamps, and removes details only three people could plausibly know, while preserving everything with evidentiary value. A second adversarial agent then tries to re-identify the author and guess their role. If it succeeds, the report goes back for another pass. For photos, Gemini vision scans the frame for self-identifying content (your own house, your vehicle, a reflection, a distinctive tattoo) and blurs it, on top of EXIF stripping.
Responder side: One anonymous report gets ignored. Fourteen reports clustered on the same outfall pipe, corroborated against Sentinel-2 imagery showing a discharge plume on matching dates, becomes a case file a regulator can act on. The console builds the dossier automatically.
Why it's strong: The adversarial re-identification loop is the memorable hook and a genuine technical merit story. It sits dead centre in Sustainability & Social Impact.
2. Voice-only lifeline for migrant and bonded labourers
Constraint: Passport confiscated, phone shared or monitored, often can't read, doesn't speak the destination country's language, doesn't know which laws apply. Forced labour runs through JAPAC's fishing fleets, construction, palm oil, and garment supply chains.
The mechanic: No app, no data, no browser history. The worker gives a missed call to a local number and gets called back. Gemini Live handles a full conversation in their mother tongue including low-resource dialects, and silently scores the conversation against the ILO's eleven indicators of forced labour. The number saves in the phone as a mobile recharge service.
Responder side: NGO caseworker console. The high-value piece is the graph: every report names an employer, a facility, a recruitment agent. Clustering surfaces the recruiters and sites generating repeat cases, which is exactly how labour exploitation is structured. Gen AI drafts the formal complaint in the destination country's legal language.
Why it's strong: Brands with supply chain exposure would pay for this, which gives you the scalability answer judges ask about.
3. Situation reports from inside a blackout
Constraint: After a landslide or cyclone, the people who know the ground truth have no network, and responders are flying blind for 48 hours.
The mechanic: Gemma runs entirely on-device. Someone speaks a 60-second description and photographs the damage; the local model compresses it into a structured, few-hundred-byte situation report that fits in an SMS or a LoRa packet. Nothing needs bandwidth. When it lands, Gemini on Cloud Run fuses hundreds of these into a deduplicated, geolocated triage map and generates resource dispatch recommendations.
Why it's strong: The Gemma-local plus Gemini-cloud split is the single best technical merit story available to you, and Kerala gives you a credible reason to have built it.
4. Water that nobody will test
Constraint: Informal settlements report contaminated supply and get ignored, because complaints are anecdotes with no data behind them.
The mechanic: A cheap colorimetric test strip and a phone photo. Gemini vision reads the strip against a reference card in frame, correcting for lighting, and converts it to actual parameter values. Residents get plain-language interpretation in their language.
Responder side: The map is the weapon. When 40 households across four streets show the same contamination signature over three weeks, the system generates a formal submission to the water utility with statistical backing and a probable-cause analysis of where the ingress is.
Why it's strong: Cheapest to demo convincingly and the easiest to get real data for in the time you have.
5. Wage theft ledger for gig and informal workers
Constraint: Workers can't prove hours worked or deductions taken, and the record lives entirely in the employer's app.
The mechanic: A worker speaks their day into the phone in their own language. Gemini builds a structured, timestamped work log from unstructured speech, and photographs of payslips or app screenshots are parsed into the same ledger. Over months this becomes an evidentiary record, with the AI computing the gap against the applicable minimum wage and generating a labour-department filing when the discrepancy crosses a threshold.

How today's phone SOS actually works
The button-press SOS (every smartphone since ~2017). Five clicks of the side button, or holding power+volume, triggers a countdown, then the phone auto-dials the local emergency number and can fire an SMS with GPS coordinates to your pre-set emergency contacts. This is a personal panic button. It requires cellular or WiFi calling to exist at that moment, requires the phone to be unlocked and physically accessible to you, and sends to your contacts, not to any public authority or NGO.
Satellite Emergency SOS (iPhone 14+, and Pixel/Android equivalents). When there's zero cell and zero WiFi, the phone connects directly to a low-earth-orbit satellite fleet (Apple uses Globalstar). The connection works under exceptional circumstances when no other means of reaching emergency services are available, requires you to be outside with a clear view of sky and horizon, and the phone starts a structured text conversation sharing Medical ID, emergency contacts, questionnaire answers, location with elevation, and battery life. It's genuinely impressive engineering, and it's the single closest thing to what you're building. att
Automatic crash/fall detection. If the phone or watch detects a severe car crash or hard fall and the person is unresponsive, it can trigger the satellite SOS automatically even without the person acting. apple
Why none of this is Haven's problem, or yours
Every one of these systems shares three assumptions that break for a victim being monitored:
The user must be free to act openly. Pressing a side button five times, or holding the phone up to the sky visibly texting "911," is an unmistakable, loud action. A woman under surveillance, a worker with a confiscated phone, a villager afraid of being identified as the person who reported the factory — none of them can perform an obvious SOS gesture without consequences worse than the emergency itself.
It goes to the wrong recipient. Button SOS goes to your personal contacts or the general police line. It has no concept of "this needs a domestic violence caseworker," "this needs an environmental regulator," or "this needs to be clustered with thirteen other anonymous reports before anyone acts." It's a single alarm, not a system that reasons about the report.
It has no memory across incidents. Apple's satellite SOS handles one call in isolation. There's no case file, no linking to a pattern, no severity triage across many reports. It's built for "I am one person, this is one emergency, right now," not for "this is evidence of an ongoing, structural harm."
There's also a coverage gap worth naming honestly: Apple's satellite feature only works in the USA, Canada, Oceania, and a handful of European countries so far, and it needs hardware that only exists in recent flagship phones. Most of your target users in JAPAC won't have it at all. androidauthority
The actual difference, stated cleanly
Built-in phone SOS is: a visible, one-shot alarm from a free person to their contacts, when infrastructure is fully broken.
Your system is: a covert or low-signal report from a constrained person, routed through Gen AI triage to the right responder, correlated with other reports over time, sent over whichever degraded channel is actually available.
Concretely, three things phones don't do that you do:
Covert capture. Haven used steganography so the act of reporting looks like nothing happened. Your disaster variant uses a missed call or a background-compressed report so the act of reporting is invisible to anyone watching the phone.
AI-mediated triage before it ever reaches a human. Apple's satellite flow is a raw conversation forwarded to a relay center. Yours classifies severity, deduplicates, and clusters before a responder ever sees it — that's the Gen AI doing real work, not just moving bytes.
Cross-report intelligence. One iPhone SOS call is one call. Your system's value compounds: report 40 lines up with reports 12 and 27 on the same location and becomes a case, not a ping.
That comparison — "phones already solve the alarm problem for a free person with working satellite hardware; we solve the reporting problem for a constrained person with none of that" — is a clean, honest line for your deck, and it preempts the first question a Google Cloud judge will ask.

Exploring sensor-based methods for detecting accidents or falls.
Yes — this is exactly the right direction, and it's also exactly what Apple's Crash Detection and Fall Detection already do at a basic level. You'd be extending that same sensor-fusion approach to more scenarios and, critically, feeding it into your triage and mesh-relay system instead of Apple's closed loop.
What the phone can sense without the person touching it
Accelerometer — measures acceleration on three axes, hundreds of times a second. A fall or crash has a distinctive signature: near-zero-g free fall, followed by a massive deceleration spike, followed by stillness. A cave fall looks different from a car crash (longer free-fall window, no seatbelt-style deceleration), and both look completely different from someone just dropping their phone.
Gyroscope — measures rotation rate. A person tumbling down a slope rotates chaotically before impact; someone who sets their phone on a table doesn't. This is what separates "phone dropped" from "body fell."
Barometer — measures air pressure, which converts to altitude. This is the one that matters most for your cave scenario specifically: a sudden altitude drop of several metres in under a second, uncorrelated with any elevator or vehicle, is a very strong falling signal that accelerometer data alone can be ambiguous about.
GPS/GNSS — as we covered, works with no network, gives you location the moment of the event.
Microphone — can catch an impact sound signature or, more usefully, catch the absence of expected sound afterward (silence following a cry, sudden lack of movement audio) as a corroborating signal.
Post-event stillness — this is the piece most fall-detection systems lean on hardest. The event itself (the spike) tells you something happened. The 30–60 seconds of near-zero motion afterward tells you the person likely isn't getting up. That combination is what separates a real fall from someone who tripped, caught themselves, and kept walking.
The architecture: on-device model, not cloud
This has to run on-device, because by definition the moment matters most when there's no connectivity — that's the whole premise of your project. So:
Gemma runs locally as a lightweight motion classifier, fed a rolling window of accelerometer + gyroscope + barometer samples (say, the last 3–5 seconds at all times, discarded continuously). It's not doing anything exotic — this is fundamentally a time-series classification problem, well within a small on-device model's capability, and you'd likely pretrain it on public fall-detection datasets (there are several academic ones) rather than training from scratch.
When the classifier crosses a confidence threshold for "fall" or "high-impact event," it doesn't fire immediately. It starts a countdown with a check-in prompt — vibrate, flash, ask "are you okay?" — because this is where every fall-detection system lives or dies on false positives. If the person cancels or the phone detects normal movement resuming, nothing happens. If there's no response and stillness continues, it escalates automatically.
Escalation reuses your entire existing pipeline. Once it decides this is real, it packages a report using the same compact schema you already designed — event type, severity inferred from impact magnitude, GPS with altitude, battery level, silence duration — and pushes it into the same store-carry-forward queue. It goes out over SMS if there's a signal, over the Bluetooth mesh if another phone passes by, over LoRa if a gateway is in range. Nothing new architecturally; the trigger is just autonomous instead of manual.
Why this genuinely differentiates you from Apple/Google's built-in version
Apple's Crash Detection and Fall Detection only escalate through Apple's own channel: call 911 directly, or Emergency SOS via satellite if no cellular exists, on very recent flagship hardware only. It's a closed, single-vendor system with no concept of the multi-hop degraded-network relay you're building, and no case-linking on the other end.
Yours does the same sensing job on any Android phone with a Gemma-class model, and the differentiator you can say out loud to judges is: detection is the commodity part — Apple solved that already. The actual unsolved problem is getting the alert out of a dead zone and into a responder's hands with enough structure to triage it, which is what your relay and Gemini fusion layer does.
The one thing you must get right, and should say explicitly
False positives are the entire game here. A fall detector that cries wolf gets disabled by users within a week — this is a documented, well-known failure mode in consumer fall-detection products. Two things buy you credibility on this point in the deck:
Your check-in countdown before escalating is not optional, it's the core design decision. And you should be honest that tuning the sensitivity/specificity tradeoff needs real accelerometer data from actual falls, which you likely won't have in four weeks — so plan to demo this with a controlled, staged drop test (phone strapped to a bag dropped down stairs, say) rather than claiming clinical-grade accuracy you haven't validated.
